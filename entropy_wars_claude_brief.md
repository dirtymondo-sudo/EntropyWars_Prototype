# Entropy Wars — Consolidated Story and Game Brief

## How to use this brief

Treat this document as the current source of truth when developing the story, dialogue, cutscenes, setting, or progression systems for *Entropy Wars*. Contradictions involving memory, slogans, or institutional history are deliberate manifestations of the Mandela effect; other contradictions should be avoided.

Keep future writing compatible with a low-budget, match-based tactical game. The story should enrich the battles without turning the project into a traditional exploration-heavy JRPG.

## Core concept

*Entropy Wars* is a satirical, esoteric, conspiratorial, psychedelic, nostalgic, and spiritual adventure with a retro PlayStation-era aesthetic.

The player is recruited by **D.O.O.R., the Department of Orthogonal Realities**, a secret bureaucracy that claims jurisdiction over **Canon Reality**, its name for baseline Earth. Recruitment standards have collapsed because reality breaches are multiplying.

DOOR rejects the popular idea of “parallel universes”: parallel lines never meet, so parallel realities could not interact. According to DOOR doctrine, realities meet at right angles. Orthogonal lines create corners, and corners create doors. This is why DOOR facilities are round and why employees treat ordinary square objects as potential hazards.

Uncontrolled thresholds now connect Earth to alternate dimensions, fiction, folklore, cryptid habitats, alien worlds, Heaven, Hell, the past, and the future. Their inhabitants are leaking into Canon Reality, causing the escalating global conflict called the **Entropy Wars**. DOOR officially denies the existence of ogres and elves; they never appear in the game because the institution considers them offensively overused.

The Men in Black are a rival organization. They consider DOOR dangerously irresponsible, while DOOR resents them—and CERN—for receiving public credit or blame for portals and Mandela effects.

## Format and gameplay

- Core combat is **4-versus-4 turn-based tactical JRPG combat on an 8-by-8 grid**.
- The roster contains roughly **100 unlockable playable characters**.
- Online PvP is the primary mode, supported by several single-player modes.
- There is no conventional overworld, dungeon crawling, or mission exploration. Limited exploration is concentrated in DOOR headquarters; its doors launch self-contained battle maps.
- Target price: **$9.99–$14.99**.

### Arena objectives

Saturnian Black Cubes are appearing around the world. Their many corners make them powerful anchors for orthogonal thresholds. Each battle can be won in three ways:

1. **Wipeout:** eliminate the opposing team or invading creatures. The threshold remains open. This reflects **Opener** philosophy.
2. **Destroy the Cube:** destroy the portal anchor and close the threshold. This reflects **Closer** philosophy.
3. **Secure three Keys:** find the scattered Keys and stabilize or control the threshold without erasing it. This reflects **HINGE** philosophy.

Keys operate on two levels. They are a tactical objective within matches, but recovered Keys also contribute to long-term progression and unlock restricted doors in headquarters. A player should retain useful Key progress even when a match ends through another victory condition.

Each map has a recognizable native enemy pool—for example, demons in Hell and angels in Heaven. Cleared maps can later receive **Code Red** anomaly events in which an unauthorized character appears somewhere they do not belong. A doorbell alert announces these events and gives players a reason to revisit maps.

## Metaphysical rules

Reality conserves both **connectivity** and **orthogonality**:

- DOOR cannot change the total number of connections between realities. Sealing a passage from Reality A to Reality B causes another passage to form elsewhere. Forcing one passage open causes another to close.
- The same principle applies to corners. Removing a corner from one place does not destroy it; the orthogonality reappears somewhere else.

This law explains the central conflict. Closers unknowingly create new openings, while Openers unknowingly cause other passages to close. Each side can therefore produce endless evidence that the other is making reality worse. Neither can win permanently.

DOOR’s greatest institutional mistake was treating displacement as elimination. Its round headquarters did not remove its corners; decades of renovations concentrated them beneath the facility.

## Ideological factions

Faction membership cuts across the employee hierarchy. Everyone knows the conflict exists, but few admit their allegiance. Openers and Closers routinely undermine each other during operations.

| Faction | Belief and virtue | Danger and hypocrisy |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **OPEN — Orthogonal Passages Exploration Network** | Doors enable refuge, freedom, knowledge, and exchange between realities. | Careless contact can contaminate or destroy both societies. Opening one passage silently closes another. |
| **CLOSE — Containment and Lockdown of Orthogonal Spatial Events** | Canon Reality and vulnerable ecosystems must be protected from cross-reality contamination. | Containment can condemn innocent outsiders and merely relocates the next breach. |
| **HINGE — Harmonic Interdimensional Neutrality and Gateway Equilibrium** | Opening and closing must be balanced because connectivity is conserved. | Secretly choosing where unavoidable disasters occur turns HINGE into an unaccountable technocracy. |

HINGE understands more than the other factions, but it is not morally correct by default. The thematic question is not simply whether doors should be open or closed. A functioning door must do both; the real question is **who gets to operate it, and who bears the consequences**.

## The player’s story

The player begins as a desperate, underqualified recruit with the rank of **Doormat**, housed in a converted janitor’s closet. As they complete missions, they rise through DOOR, gain access to new parts of headquarters, and uncover increasingly classified versions of reality.

The player grew up believing that both parents died in incidents built around opposite kinds of doors:

- One died in a house fire because a closed door prevented escape.
- The other died during a home invasion made possible because he accidentally left a door open.

The coherent underlying truth is that both parents worked for DOOR and represented opposing philosophies. The first was a Closer who knowingly died holding an extradimensional threshold shut during the “fire.” The second was connected to the Openers and died after allowing someone or something through; DOOR recorded the breach as a mundane home invasion. Whether DOOR merely concealed their deaths or deliberately caused them remains a central mystery.

This gives the player “one foot in the door” before recruitment and makes the faction conflict personal. Late in the story, a door in the forbidden corridor leads to the player’s childhood home. The player may witness their younger self mourning, or glimpse a reality in which both parents survived. This should be the emotional center of the story, not merely another conspiracy reveal.

By the climax, the player learns that no faction can abolish the crisis and that DOOR has long preserved Canon Reality by exporting danger elsewhere. The final conflict should force a decision about responsibility and control rather than offer a simple “open everything” or “close everything” solution.

## DOOR headquarters

Headquarters is the player hub: a bureaucratic, increasingly impossible version of Peach’s Castle, the Backrooms, *House of Leaves*, and the Overlook Hotel. It is navigable, but missions themselves remain match-based.

### Architecture and progression

- Almost every room and corridor is curved. Tables are round, ID cards are beveled, monitors have rounded corners, and every sheet of paper has at least one corner torn off.
- The ironic DOOR logo is a cube and appears everywhere.
- The tutorial/training room is the facility’s only approved square room because combat requires an 8-by-8 grid. It is notoriously prone to leaks.
- Mission thresholds sit in containment bays and look like ordinary doors that are absurdly wrong for their surroundings: a motel door glowing with sunlight, a medieval portcullis in concrete, a suburban closet door, a wet submarine bulkhead, or an EXIT door labeled with an unknown destination.
- Door lights use one consistent code: **red** means locked or actively dangerous, **yellow** means accessible but unstable or uncleared, and **green** means stabilized/mastered.
- A mastered mission door becomes a safe internal shortcut to a deeper section of headquarters. The original battle map remains replayable through the training system. Exact mastery requirements can be tuned later, but should combine story completion with map-specific objectives rather than demand PvP wins.
- More rooms and thresholds appear as the player’s clearance rises. Some doors are barricaded, taped off, or entirely absent until later chapters.

Useful hub locations include a medical unit functioning like a classic JRPG church or Pokémon Center, a large specimen/recruitment hall displaying encountered characters, containment bays, research offices, archives, and the training room. The existing `r2/Assets/terrain/tilefloor.png` can be used for flooring; doors can have wooden planks, gunmetal.png, but most will probably require brand new assets. 

### Unreliable layout

Headquarters should remain perfectly consistent long enough for players to memorize it. Then tiny, unannounced changes begin: a vending machine moves, a hallway lengthens, Research gains a fourth door, or an employee insists Containment has always been counterclockwise. Eventually departments exchange positions. The circular layout makes these Mandela effects difficult to verify.

### H-Wing

Late in the game, the player discovers **H-Wing**, a forbidden straight corridor beneath the facility. It contains perfect right angles, beige carpet, fluorescent lights, cubicles, and many doors. It is older and more stable than the round headquarters. This would essentially replace the Backrooms map. 

H-Wing is the physical accumulation of every corner DOOR removed during decades of institutional “corner-cutting.” Its existence proves that the agency displaced the danger rather than solving it. Its ordinary stability raises a further question: if right angles inherently cause leaks, why did DOOR originally operate from a rectangular facility?

## Rank and door progression

Ideology is separate from rank. The basic hierarchy is:

1. **Doormats:** interns, probationary hires, and disposable assistants.
2. **Doorstops:** junior permanent staff who can obstruct requests but rarely make decisions.
3. **Knockers:** field agents who investigate new thresholds and make first contact.
4. **Keyholders:** trusted staff with meaningful clearance.
5. **Gatekeepers:** managers who control access to people, information, and realities.
6. **The Doorman:** the mysterious title used for the agency’s highest authority.

The player’s office door visibly represents promotion: a warped janitor’s-closet door becomes a cheap hollow-core door, an institutional wired-glass door, a security door with keycard access, frosted executive glass, a biometric seamless threshold, and finally an advanced opening that barely resembles a door. In contrast, the Doorman has an ancient battered wooden door. Nobody comments on it.

## Unreliable motto and Mandela effects

There is supposedly one official DOOR motto, but its wording changes with reality. The protagonist gradually becomes able to remember versions that other employees cannot. Openers preserve archives beginning with **DO**; Closers preserve equally convincing versions beginning with **DON’T**.

Use three principal forms rather than an unlimited list:

- Early: **DO Observe Other Realities.**
- Middle: **DON’T. Open. Observe. Report.** Employees insist this was taught during orientation.
- Late/crisis state: **DO OPEN OUR REALITY.**

The motto appears on posters, plaques, loading screens, manuals, and beneath the DOOR seal. Its current form acts as a subtle reality barometer. No surviving version is necessarily the original. When asked for the true motto, a HINGE member says, “Depends which side of the door you’re asking from.”

The official organization name remains **Department of Orthogonal Realities**. “Doors Open Other Realities” is an unofficial backronym or recruitment joke, not a second formal name. The running bureaucratic joke is that the D can mean “Do” or “Don’t,” depending on institutional convenience.

Every recruit receives a standard protractor, and veteran agents still carry theirs. “Check your corners” is both a safety rule and a threat. A measurement of exactly 90 degrees should provoke immediate fear.

"It's 90 degrees." / "Oh shit."

Use door language for institutional humor—lock, key, knob, handle, frame, knocking, doormat, front door, back door, revolving door, trapdoor, cutting corners, unhinged—but sparingly enough that serious scenes retain weight.

Core aphorisms include:

- “A door that can never open is a wall. A door that can never close is a hole.”
- “When one door closes, another opens.” This is a literal statement of the conservation law.
- “I wouldn’t mislead you. I’m an open door.” / “You mean book?” / “…Yes.”

Doorbells signal institutional alerts; slow knocking signals something intimate or dangerous on the other side.

## Story delivery and scope

Chapter 1, **Minimum Qualifications**, is the tutorial. After it, the full game opens and the main story advances through milestones earned in any eligible mode:

**Play matches → earn Story Progress → reach a threshold → view a post-match scene → receive the next milestone.**

Story advancement must not require PvP victories. Winning should accelerate progress, not gate it. A baseline scoring model is:

- Complete a match: +10 Story Progress
- Win: +5 bonus
- Complete a special objective: +3
- Use a story-relevant character: +2

Other valid contributors include XP, turns played, objectives, characters or team combinations used, and damage, healing, or support performed. Optional scenes can react to favorite characters, specific pairings, relationships, achievements, and play history.

Target **10–15 chapters**, **50–80 core scenes**, and preferably **60–75 scenes totaling 1.5–2.5 hours**. Normal scenes should run 30–90 seconds, important scenes 2–3 minutes, and major reveals or finales 3–6 minutes. Reserve expensive cinematics for the opening, major reveals, chapter finales, and ending; most scenes should use reusable models, portraits, animations, environments, camera work, dialogue UI, music, doorbell sounds, and knocking sounds.

## Narrative spine

### Act I — Recruitment and containment

Open with a battered VHS recruitment tape. It explains orthogonal realities through cheerful training graphics: parallel lines never meet and receive a “JURISDICTION DECLINED” stamp; two perpendicular lines form a door; an entity waves from the intersection; a square sandwich triggers an alarm. The narrator explains that the viewer either applied, was referred, was abducted, or was miscategorized by HR, then welcomes the player as “what remains of our standards.” Three knocks come from a darkening corner, followed by the instruction, “Please do not turn around.” The player turns around, initiating the tutorial breach.

The player becomes a Doormat, learns the three arena objectives, handles early anomalies, and participates in the first supposedly successful destruction of a Black Cube in Antarctica. Early clues connect the parents to DOOR.

### Act II — Competing realities

The player gains rank and visits increasingly impossible locations. Faction sabotage makes it clear that every mission is also an internal proxy war. Area 51 presents the Men in Black’s competing account, in which DOOR is the reckless conspiracy. Camelot reveals that the Round Table was early HINGE technology designed without a privileged side. On the Moon, a freestanding door and impossible footprints contradict the official history of lunar missions. Heaven, Hell, the Backrooms, and other locations broaden the conflict without providing a single authoritative cosmology.

Meanwhile, headquarters shifts subtly, mastered doors become shortcuts, Code Red anomalies place characters in the wrong worlds, and the motto changes. The player begins remembering realities that everyone else has forgotten.

### Act III — The displaced corners

Evidence points to the conservation law and to HINGE’s hidden role in deciding where displaced breaches occur. The player discovers H-Wing, learns that DOOR’s safety measures merely exported orthogonality and suffering, and reaches the childhood-home threshold. The truth about both parents reframes the Openers and Closers as inherited halves of the player’s conflict.

The crisis-state motto becomes “DO OPEN OUR REALITY,” suggesting that the institution, the Doorman, or reality itself is preparing to expose Canon Reality completely. The climax should bring all three arena philosophies into the plot and make the player confront who is entitled to choose which worlds are connected, sacrificed, or protected.

## Location anchors

- **Antarctica:** isolation, preservation, and the first Black Cube that DOOR claims to have destroyed.
- **Area 51:** the Men in Black’s rival version of events; they regard DOOR as the dangerous organization.
- **Camelot:** the Round Table was built deliberately as early HINGE technology with no privileged side.
- **The Moon:** a door standing without a wall, plus footprints from missions that officially never happened.
- **Heaven and Hell:** opposing mythic ecosystems treated by DOOR as ordinary jurisdictions and recurring combat maps.
- **Desert threshold:** an isolated, visually absurd door where no structure should exist.
- **H-Wing/childhood home:** the late-game metaphysical and emotional center.

## Tone rules for future writing

1. Play the absurd bureaucracy straight. Employees discuss impossible horrors with procedural irritation.
2. Let the comedy support rather than erase the danger. Parent scenes, refugee dilemmas, and faction consequences should be sincere.
3. Make institutional claims sound confident but not necessarily true. DOOR controls the definition of “canon,” not objective reality.
4. Reveal lore through altered spaces, props, mission objectives, and conflicting records—not only exposition.
5. Keep every faction sympathetic, compromised, and capable of harm.
6. Use retro visual limitations intentionally: VHS artifacts, low-poly silhouettes, looping office animations, abrupt loading transitions, and uncanny reused assets can become part of the aesthetic.
7. Do not introduce ogres or elves. Their absence is a recurring institutional joke, not a mystery requiring resolution.

## Intentionally unresolved questions

These should remain open until the detailed chapter outline is written:

- Who or what is the Doorman, and why is their door older than headquarters?
- Did DOOR order either parent’s death, knowingly sacrifice them, or only falsify the aftermath?  Mondo here, they definitely just falsified the aftermath as routine collateral damage. 
- Is “Saturnian” the Black Cubes’ true origin or merely DOOR’s esoteric classification?
- Why was the original rectangular facility stable?
- Is Canon Reality genuinely special, or simply the reality with the bureaucracy powerful enough to declare itself canon?
- What exact combination of story completion and map objectives constitutes mastery? - Probably winning on that map with all win conditions, or maybe recruiting/unlocking all creatures on that map to your roster. 
- What final choice, if any, does the player make about the distribution of unavoidable breaches? - Maybe the player becomes the Doorman, and the Doorman was the janitor all along.
